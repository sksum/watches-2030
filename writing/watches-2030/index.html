Watches, 2030

Nov 2026

While thinking about the future workflow in a mixed reality world, I couldn't help but assume different timelines, one being the near future and the other fantasy case, put here in the end.

I'm deeply interested in watches. On several occasions, I've enthusiastically (and almost certainly unsolicitedly) overexplained the intricacies of precision engineering, mechanical movements and how complications is a good thing here. I've often caught my friends' faces slowly transitioning from polite curiosity to mentally planning what they're having for lunch, but I soldier on regardless.

During the few times I have been able to entice someone into understanding what I am trying to say, I hypothesize its because of two things 1. The audience has some basic level of curiosity regarding the topic and 2. I explain only 1 thing and 1 thing well. 1 This requires a level of control and patience that I do not possess. and there is another possible factor that I will talk about at the end of this one.

Thinking about how the future mixed reality experiences would look like and focusing on education and knowledge sharing, be it between an AI agent and a solo user or a multi-user workflow; I have a feeling it will look very similar overall. I'm aware that imagining remote presense of humans will remind you of the infamous zuck's metaverse announcement. The future

How and where will these virtual objects realize themselves into reality is an interesting puzzle governed by people's wants, technological quality of the XR wearables, ease of making software 2 As of Nov 2026, I feel this case is already solved. , R&D budgets at incumbent companies, economic incentives and obvious from the amount of things I mentioned, also very hard. One fundamental assumption I make is the amount of physical as well as virtual things people would want is unlimited.

The room

It is a room that a person can only enter when they have real need of it.

Dobby, describing the Room of Requirement

I was inspired by the Room of Requirement from Harry Potter, a room that transforms into whatever its inhabitant needs. I imagine mixed reality working in a similar way. Rather than looking at AR experiences as travelling through time, it's more accurate to visualise them as transforming your existing room into a room from a different place or time. Indoor spaces would become increasingly minimal. If monitors, TVs, keyboards and even computer interfaces become virtual, why do we need a desk? Why do we need a desk chair? Why do we need a desk fan? I'm sure if you keep going, all you really need is a bed to sleep on and a recliner or sofa so you don't mess up your spinal cord and gravity. A recliner is all you need. Frat guys always lived in the future.

For watches, this is a huge leveller. The nicest rooms I will never be invited into are exclusive watch showrooms, the Tokyo boutiques where they serve you tea before they open the vault, and the Geneva showrooms with collections I might never get to see. I could walk into one of these virtual spaces, approach a display case and ask to see a particular watch. I could rotate the case, open it, inspect the finishing on a calibre I could never afford and enlarge the movement to understand how its components interact. An AI agent could accompany me, answering questions as I explore. Access is the first half of education, and for the first time, the showroom's doors are open to anyone curious enough.

The desk

The showroom lets me see watches. The desk lets me understand them. I imagine sitting beside a watchmaker, except this person could be from 1910, the Second World War, or today. The desk transforms with the period: wooden drawers, brass tools and paper sketches in an old Swiss atelier, or digital microscopes, precision instruments and calibration equipment in a modern workshop. I'd like to make this an interactive experience built around three stories: designing a watch for a Second World War soldier or pilot, designing one for a physician, and finally making one for myself, with ridiculous complications and unusual case shapes. Each story introduces different engineering constraints, and the environment, tools and watchmaker change accordingly.

Across all three stories, I should be able to pick up components, disassemble movements, rotate gears, stop animations and see what happens when I change something. There is one principle I want to preserve throughout: explaining one thing at a time. I know from personal experience how easily an explanation can become a discussion about five different things before the listener has understood the first. Start with the balance wheel, show how it oscillates, introduce the escapement, and then demonstrate how the two interact. I should be able to freeze the mechanism, isolate a component and resume the animation when I'm ready. The objective isn't to finish a tutorial; it's to understand one idea well enough to move on to the next. 3 Built by shxwat (mechanical-watch-threejs, MIT) — no model file, no textures; everything procedural. The model a learner pokes at should be honest machinery, not a render.

The demonstration

The desk is where I learn, but most of my enthusiasm about watches happens in conversation. It usually starts with me showing someone a watch and explaining something I find interesting about it. Sometimes they ask a question, sometimes they get curious, and sometimes I can almost physically see them wondering how much longer they have to listen before they can leave. The important part of these conversations is that both people are reacting to the same object. We point at things, interrupt each other, change our explanations and discover details neither of us had initially noticed. Mixed reality should preserve this spontaneity, even when one person is remote or the explanation comes from an AI agent. Both people should be able to see and manipulate the same movement, point to individual components and observe each other's reactions.

I imagine the experience preserving facial expressions, gaze and gestures at a quality where a small frown or a nod is not lost. A 2019 Berkeley study, Affordance Analysis of Virtual and Augmented Reality Mediated Communication, compared face-to-face, video, VR and holographic communication, highlighting the importance of preserving gestures and facial micro-expressions in communication. The AI agent should use these signals to decide how to teach. If I'm confused, it should stop and explain the mechanism differently. If I understand something immediately, it should move on. If I've mentally moved on to lunch, it should probably recognise that too. The objective shouldn't be to maximise the amount of information delivered, but to recognise whether the other person is actually interested and learning. Curiosity is difficult to manufacture, but surprisingly easy to lose when someone refuses to notice that you've stopped paying attention.

2040

This is the fantasy case from the first paragraph. By 2040, I imagine the room no longer requiring me to explicitly tell it what I want. It should understand what I'm curious about and furnish itself accordingly. I take my watch off, put it on the table and start wondering how its movement works. The room replaces the table with a watchmaker's bench, and a watchmaker appears beside me. The movement is reproduced at a scale where I can inspect every component. I ask about the balance spring, and the room isolates it, enlarges it and demonstrates how it behaves. The room is no longer a collection of interfaces; it is an environment that responds to intent.

The interesting part is that these experiences should persist beyond a single person. I could invite a friend in Tokyo, while I'm sitting in New York, and the two of us would be standing beside the same virtual desk, looking at the same movement. We could leave the mechanism partially disassembled, come back the following evening and continue where we stopped. An AI agent could remember what we explored and what we wanted to investigate next. Thursday is watch club, except the room furnishes itself. Restoration nights get a Swiss atelier from 1911; teaching nights get a movement the size of a cathedral, with the balance wheel oscillating overhead. Someone dialing in from Osaka is simply there, full size, flinching at the tick. I still have my physical watch. I don't think virtual experiences replace the physical object. They give me access to things the physical object alone cannot provide.
